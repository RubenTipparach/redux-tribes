Beam Shader

A beam is currently one THREE.Line in flat cyan, one pixel wide at every range, with an opacity that fades over its second. It reads as a diagram. Here are three shaders that read as a weapon, against that line, moving, at the two ranges that matter.

What is being decided here. Which of the three, and whether the extra draw is worth it. Nothing below is wired into the game. A beam cannot change an outcome, so none of this crosses the boundary and none of it is hashed: the core already decided what was hit, and this is only how the shot is drawn.

1 The four, side by side

Same shot, same second, four treatments. The camera pulls back through the loop so each is seen at inspection range and at map range, because a beam that looks good filling the screen and vanishes at 300 units is a beam a player never sees in a real turn.

A

The line, as shipped

One THREE.Line, flat cyan, additive. One pixel wide whatever the range and whatever the gun. It is the control, not a candidate.

B

Cored lance

A camera facing quad with a white hot core and a coloured falloff across its width. The one change that does most of the work: a beam gains a thickness the range can act on.

C

Cored, travelling, flared

B, plus energy running along the beam and a flare at the muzzle. The travel says which way the shot is going, which a symmetric beam never does.

D

Lance and shock ring

C, plus a ring at the far end that expands and fades on impact. The end of a beam is where the interesting thing happened and it is currently unmarked.

2 Why a quad and not a line

A line's width is a lie on most hardware: WebGL ignores linewidth on nearly every driver, so a beam is one pixel at four units and one pixel at four hundred. That is why the current beam reads as a diagram, and it is not something a nicer colour fixes.

A camera facing quad gives width in WORLD units, so a beam thins with distance the way a real thing does, and it gives a coordinate ACROSS the beam, which is what a core and a falloff need. Two triangles a shot, and the quad is rebuilt per frame from the two endpoints the event already carries: no geometry is kept between frames, so scrubbing a turn backwards runs the beams backwards with everything else.

The floor is the finding. A world width alone vanishes at range: 0.34 units seen from 250 units out is a third of a pixel, so the beam a player is meant to be watching is the one they cannot see. Pull the camera back with the floor at zero and the three shaders disappear while the flat line survives, which is the one thing the line is good at. The width is therefore the world width OR a floor in screen pixels, whichever is wider: close in the world width wins and a beam has real thickness, far out the floor holds it at a readable two or three pixels. Two and a half is where a beam stops disappearing without becoming a stripe across the map.

All four are additive with depth writing off, which is what makes beams light each other rather than cut holes, and is what the blasts already do. The bloom pass thresholds just under one after tone mapping, so the core is authored above that and the falloff below it: the middle of a beam blooms and its edges do not, which is the difference between a glow and a smear.
