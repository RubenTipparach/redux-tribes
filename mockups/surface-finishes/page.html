Hull Finishes and Windows

A hull is a lattice of cells and every face is a flat rectangle of one colour. That reads at map range and it is bare up close, which is exactly where the console now takes a player. Two additions fix it without touching a single rule: a normal map chosen per palette colour, and a window decal painted per cell.

What is being decided here. Which of these nine finishes and six windows are worth having, and whether the map's own lighting shows them off well enough to be worth the bytes. Nothing below is wired into the game and nothing is in web/public/. Say which ones survive and they get built.

1 The nine finishes

Each plate is three cells by three cells, so the tiling is visible: one repeat of the texture per hull cell, whatever size the merged quad turns out to be. The light sweeps, because a normal map that is never raked is a picture of a pattern rather than a surface.

The thing I expected to go wrong, and did not. The map is lit by a hemisphere light at 1.6 and one key at 1.1, and a hemisphere light is close to ambient, which is exactly what a normal map cannot show: with no direction to the light there is no shading to perturb. It turns out the key alone carries them. Press Finishes off to take every normal map away and put it back: the seams, rivets and ribs are plainly the difference, under the shading model that ships today and with no new light and no material change. Specular shading swaps Lambert for Phong to show what a highlight would add on top, which is more, and is a separate decision with its own cost.

3 Windows are a decal, and the paint stays underneath

A window is three maps, not one. A recess in the normals, a glow in the emission, and between them the one that matters: a glass map that MULTIPLIES the hull's paint down to nearly nothing where the glass is. Without it an unlit pane is plain armour in the armour's own colour, which is a rectangle of hull where a window should be, and a lit pane reads as the hull's colour with a wash over it rather than as light coming out. The frame keeps the paint, so paint the hull Karisen rust and it is a window in a rust coloured hull rather than a sticker. Two window cells side by side merge into one quad carrying two windows, because the decal tiles like every other texture here.

Press Lights out above to kill the key light and see what the emission alone is doing. Not every pane is lit: one dark pane in four is what makes a row of windows read as a ship with somebody aboard rather than as a texture.

4 What it costs

texturewhat it isbytes

All 128 x 128, every dimension a power of two (GUIDELINES 4). A design wears one finish per colour and realistically one or two colours, so a hull costs one or two of these plus one window pair, not the whole set. They are separate files rather than an atlas on purpose: a finish has to TILE once per cell across a greedy quad that may be twenty cells wide, and repeat wrapping repeats a whole image rather than one tile of it.

5 What this does not touch

Nothing here changes an outcome, so nothing here crosses the boundary. Meshes, materials and lighting are the client's business; the core decides what happens. A finish is stored with the design beside paint, and like paint it is not passed to ft_match_new and not in the state hash. Two seats that disagreed about whether a hull is riveted or ablative would still resolve the same turn to the same hash.

One integration cost is real and worth stating up front: a window cell cannot merge with a neighbour that is not also a window, so painting them adds quads. How many depends entirely on whether they are CONTIGUOUS. A run of window cells side by side merges into one quad that carries one window per cell, which is nearly free; nine windows scattered a few cells apart shatter the long armour rectangles they sit in and cost about seven quads each. The hull above uses a contiguous strip and reports the figure under the controls as you change it.
